## Problem

Roughly a billion people cannot prove who they are. A refugee whose papers were lost in flight, a child born in a camp that no registry recognizes, a stateless minority a government refuses to enroll, or someone whose national civil registry was destroyed in a war all hit the same wall: without a foundational identity they cannot open an account, receive aid in their own name, enroll in school, or claim a legal status. The institution that would normally issue the identity is often the one that is absent, hostile, or the source of the displacement. Aid agencies build their own enrollment databases to cope, but each one is a silo a person cannot carry between agencies or across a border, and a host state can demand it or shut it down.

## Solution

An identity a person accumulates and holds themselves, built from attestations that several independent parties can issue: a UN agency confirming registration, an NGO confirming biometric enrollment, a clinic confirming a birth, peers vouching through a web of trust. No single attestation has to come from a state that will not cooperate, and the bundle grows more credible as more independent parties sign it. The person controls disclosure and carries the identity across agencies and borders in their own wallet. A workable starting point is a single displaced population served by one or two aid organizations already doing biometric enrollment, issuing their existing registration as a portable attestation the person keeps rather than a record locked in the agency's database. Bank onboarding, school enrollment, and cross-agency reuse follow once the attestation is portable.

## Why Ethereum

For this population the issuer of last resort, the state, is precisely the party that is missing or adversarial, so an identity that depends on any single authority's permission fails the people who need it most. A neutral chain lets attestations from many independent issuers compose into a usable identity that no host government, no single agency, and no funder can revoke or hold hostage, and that survives when an agency loses funding or a regime changes. Censorship resistance and self-custody are not nice-to-haves here: they are the difference between an identity a person keeps and one a hostile institution can erase.
